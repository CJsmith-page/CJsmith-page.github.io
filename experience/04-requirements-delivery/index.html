Requirements and Prototype Delivery

I delivered the full requirements set across three projects and kept a fast-moving, cross-team automation prototype on track.

Business Analyst

View repository on GitHub →
  • ProblemThree simulation projects needed requirements written from scratch, while an automation prototype moved fast across three teams with shifting tasks.
  • What I didWrote user stories, process workflows and functional specs, built Power BI dashboards for stakeholder reviews, and tracked every cross-team dependency in Jira.
  • ResultRequirements delivered for all three projects, and two major blockers caught early and resolved before they hit the sprint goal.

The situation

I ran two workstreams in parallel. The three projects were simulations: realistic business scenarios that needed the complete set of requirements a real project would have. The automation prototype was live work, with tasks changing quickly across three teams.

How I delivered it

  1. Started from stakeholder needs

    Began each project by capturing what the stakeholders needed before writing any requirement.

    Why: Requirements written from a solution rather than a need tend to build the wrong thing well.

  2. Wrote the full requirements set

    Produced user stories, process workflows and functional specifications for all three projects.

    Why: Each serves a different reader. Stories guide the build, workflows show the process end to end, and specs pin down the detail developers and testers need.

  3. Used dashboards to drive stakeholder reviews

    Analysed datasets for two business cases and built Power BI dashboards for the stakeholder presentations.

    Why: Stakeholders give better feedback when they can see the evidence, not just read about it.

  4. Tracked every task and dependency on the prototype

    Kept 6+ evolving tasks in Jira, tagged every dependency across the three teams and checked daily what was blocking what.

    Why: When tasks keep changing, dependencies are the first thing to get lost, and a hidden dependency is a blocker waiting to happen.

  5. Flagged blockers early and agreed owners

    Surfaced two major blockers before they affected the sprint, and got owners agreed for each.

    Why: A blocker found early is a conversation. Found at the end of a sprint, it's a missed goal.

The call I made

Track dependencies, not just tasks. With tasks shifting daily, a task list alone would have looked fine right up until something stalled. Tagging every cross-team dependency is what made the two blockers visible in time.
